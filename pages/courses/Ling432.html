Psychology of Language

How speakers and listeners process language, with little effort and almost no awareness of how it’s done — speech perception, word recognition, sentence comprehension and production, reading and writing, and what happens when the system breaks down.

About This Course

This upper-division course is an elective for students in Linguistics, Philosophy, and Psychology, typically taken by juniors and seniors. It’s an introduction to psycholinguistics, a branch of cognitive science that asks how language is processed by speakers and listeners — something most of us do for most of our waking hours without any real sense of how it happens. A thorough understanding requires marrying basic linguistic facts to psychological theory: what the components of language are, and how those components are stored in, and retrieved from, the mind.

Topics covered typically include the nature of language, speech perception, word recognition, sentence comprehension and production, reading and writing, processing in a compromised system, and language acquisition. By the end of the course, students will be able to identify the individual and overlapping tasks of the language processing system, assess and discuss scholarly work on language processing, compare and replicate methodologies used to investigate psycholinguistic phenomena, and demonstrate that understanding in written reports.

There are no exams in this course. Reading quizzes (weekly, weeks 1–7) and lecture quizzes (weeks 11–16) are administered on D2L, objective-style, with two attempts each and no retakes after that. The literature review paper stands in for a midterm; the research proposal is due at the end of the term. Participation is graded on attendance plus weekly discussion posts, which require both an original post and a reply to a peer.
